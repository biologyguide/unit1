Intro to Macromolecules

What the big molecules of life are, which elements they're made of, and how small pieces link up to build big ones.

What is a macromolecule?

Macro means big. A water molecule (H2O) has only 3 atoms. One protein molecule can have thousands. Your notes count a molecule as a macromolecule once it has more than 200 atoms.

Living things are built mostly from four kinds of macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids (slides 4 and 7).

They are organic compounds

In chemistry, organic has nothing to do with farming. It means the molecule contains both carbon and hydrogen. All four macromolecules are organic, and carbon forms the backbone of every one of them.

Quick check: look for a C and an H in the formula. One without the other is not enough. CO₂ is the classic trick question.

Monomers build polymers

Break the words apart: mono = one, poly = many, -mer = part. A monomer is one small piece. A polymer is many of those pieces bonded into a long chain. Every macromolecule is a polymer built from its own kind of monomer (see the Monomer row in the Big Four table).

Analogies for slides 12–13

  • Monomers are like LEGO bricks and polymers are like the LEGO building, because you use many bricks to build it. From your slides
  • Monomers are like beads and polymers are like a necklace, because many beads strung together make the necklace.
  • Monomers are like train cars and polymers are like the whole train, because the cars link into one long chain.
  • Monomers are like letters and polymers are like words, because letters in a different order spell a different word. This one fits proteins especially well.

Common mix-ups

  • "Found in living things" is not the same as organic. Water is in every cell, but it has no carbon, so it isn't organic.
  • A monomer alone isn't a macromolecule. One glucose (24 atoms) is a monomer. Starch, made of many glucose units, is the macromolecule.
  • Carbon alone doesn't make it organic. You need carbon and hydrogen.

Carbohydrates & Lipids

Macromolecules #1 and #2. Both are made of only C, H and O, but they do different jobs: carbs give quick energy and structure, lipids store energy, insulate and build membranes.

Carbohydrates

Sugars and starches

Carbohydrates ("carbs") are sugars and chains of sugars. Glucose is the classic example. Read its formula like a recipe: C₆H₁₂O₆ means 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms.

ElementsCHO

Spot a sugar by its name. Slide 6 lists fructose, glucose, lactose, dextrose and maltose. What do you notice? The names of sugars always end in -ose.

Monosaccharides build polysaccharides

Saccharide means sugar. So a monosaccharide is one sugar and a polysaccharide is many sugars bonded in a chain. It's the same monomer → polymer idea from Lesson 1.

  • Starch: the complex carb in starchy foods: bread, cereals, pasta, rice, potatoes, beans and chestnuts (slide 4). It's how plants store sugar. Extra
  • Glycogen: a chain of many glucose units (slide 8). It's how animals, including you, store glucose, mostly in the liver and muscles. Extra
  • Cellulose: forms the cell walls of plants (slide 10).

What carbs do: energy and structure

Function 1 · Energy

Your body turns glucose into quick energy (slide 9). Carbs are the fuel your cells reach for first.

Function 2 · Structure
  • Cellulose forms the cell walls of plants (slide 10).
  • Chitin forms the exoskeleton of insects and the cell walls of fungi (slide 11). The slide also pictures crab and lobster shells and mushrooms.

Packet answer: The two main functions of carbohydrates are providing quick energy and providing structure.

Lipids

Fats and oils

Lipids are the greasy, oily molecules. They have the same elements as carbs (C, H, O) but far fewer oxygen atoms. The fat molecule from Lesson 1, C₈₈H₁₆₄O₁₀, has 164 hydrogens and only 10 oxygens. Lipids also don't mix with water, which is why oil floats in a puddle. Extra

ElementsCHO

Saturated
  • Only single bonds between carbons
  • Example: butter
  • The "bad" fat in your notes
  • Straight chains pack tightly, so it's solid at room temperature Extra
Unsaturated
  • Has double bonds between carbons
  • Example: olive oil
  • The "healthy" fat in your notes

Building blocks: fatty acids and glycerol

What lipids do

Slide 19

Energy storage

Lipids store 2× more energy than carbohydrates. Carbs are pocket cash for quick spending; fat is the savings account.

Slide 20

Insulation

A layer of fat under the skin helps keep us warm by holding body heat in, like the seal's blubber pictured on the slide.

Slide 21

Cell membrane

The phospholipid bilayer (two layers of lipids) forms the cell membrane and controls what goes in and out of cells, in both animal and plant cells.

Lipids and homeostasis

Homeostasis means keeping conditions inside your body steady (temperature, energy supply, what's inside each cell), no matter what is happening outside. Here are model answers for the three packet sentences:

  • Energy storage maintains homeostasis because the body has stored fuel to keep cells running even when you haven't eaten.
  • Insulation maintains homeostasis because a layer of fat holds in heat, keeping body temperature stable when it's cold outside.
  • The cell membrane maintains homeostasis because it controls what goes in and out of the cell, keeping the inside of the cell stable.

Common mix-ups

  • Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all polysaccharides made of glucose. Plants store starch, animals store glycogen, and cellulose builds plant cell walls.
  • Carbs and lipids have the same elements (C, H, O). Tell them apart by their jobs and examples, not their elements.
  • Saturated = single bonds. Unsaturated is the one with double bonds.
  • Glucose is the monomer (a monosaccharide). Don't call it a polysaccharide.

Lesson 3Proteins & Nucleic Acids 26 TR · 32 slides · Packet pp. 13–14

Proteins & Nucleic Acids

Macromolecules #3 and #4. Proteins do almost every job in your body. Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) hold the instructions for building those proteins.

Proteins

Amino acids are the building blocks

Think of the 20 amino acids as a 20-letter alphabet. A protein is a "word" that can be hundreds of letters long, in any order. That's why there's a practically endless number of different proteins.

The picture on slide 3 shows what an amino acid looks like: a nitrogen (N) end, a central carbon, an acid end, and a part labeled R. All 20 amino acids share the same backbone. The R group is the part that's different in each one, and it's what makes the 20 amino acids different from each other.

Sequence → structure → function

Order of amino acids→Shape of the protein→Its job

Amino acids link into a chain called a polypeptide. The chain then folds into a 3-D shape, and that shape decides what the protein can do. Change the order of amino acids and you change the shape, and the protein may stop working.

Your packet adds: because proteins do so many jobs, an incorrectly shaped protein caused by a mutation is often the reason behind genetic diseases.

The picture on slide 1 shows the levels of folding: primary (the amino acid sequence), secondary (coils and folds), tertiary (the full 3-D shape) and quaternary (several chains working together). Extra

ElementsCHONS

Remember it: carbs and lipids are just CHO. Proteins add N (nitrogen) and often S (sulfur).

Nucleic acids

DNA and RNA: the genetic material

Nucleic acids store and pass on genetic information. Every cell has them, so an apple, a steak or a leaf of lettuce all contain DNA. DNA has two strands twisted into a ladder shape (the "double helix"). RNA has one strand.

Nucleotides: three parts

ElementsCHONP

Slide 20's diagram labels the sugar a pentose sugar: a sugar with 5 carbons (penta = five). In DNA it's deoxyribose; in RNA it's ribose.

Remember it: nucleic acids are the only macromolecule in your notes with P, and the P comes from the Phosphate.

Base pairing: A–T and C–G

Remember it: Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage.

Writing a complementary strand

"Complementary" means the partner strand. Go letter by letter and swap each base for its partner: A ↔ T, C ↔ G. Don't copy the strand and don't flip its order.

What DNA does

DNA (instructions)→order of amino acids→protein shape→protein's job

Slide 24's diagram shows this loop. The order of bases in DNA is a code for the order of amino acids in a protein. Since sequence decides shape and shape decides job, DNA ends up controlling what your proteins can do.

That's how the genetic code shows up as your traits. Slide 25 pictures traits like attached earlobes, tongue rolling, dimples, freckles, curly hair, being right-handed, a cleft chin and seeing red and green.

DNA vs. RNA

DNARNA
StrandsDouble stranded (twisted ladder)Single stranded
SugarDeoxyriboseRibose
BasesA, T, C, GA, U, C, G

Same (slide 30): both are nucleic acids, both are made of nucleotides, and both have the bases C, G and A.
Different (slide 31): DNA is double stranded and RNA is single stranded; they have different sugars; DNA uses thymine while RNA uses uracil.

Common mix-ups

  • Nucleotide, nucleic acid, nucleus. A nucleotide is the monomer. A nucleic acid (DNA, RNA) is the polymer. The nucleus is a part of the cell where DNA is kept.
  • Thymine is only in DNA, uracil is only in RNA. If you see a U, it's RNA.
  • Complementary isn't the same as identical. The partner of ACT is TGA, not ACT.
  • Enzymes aren't a fifth macromolecule. They're proteins.

Enzymes

The proteins that make chemical reactions in your body happen fast: how they work, what can stop them, and why they can be used again and again.

Enzymes are proteins

Enzymes are protein job #6 from Lesson 3. Like every protein, an enzyme's job depends on its shape. Keep that in mind; it explains everything else in this lesson.

Activation energy

Picture pushing a boulder over a hill to get it rolling down the other side. The height of the hill is the activation energy. An enzyme is like digging the hill down: the boulder still ends up in the same place, but it takes much less of a push, so it happens much faster.

Slide 3's graph uses a real reaction: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, the reaction your cells use to get energy out of sugar. The products end up lower than the reactants because energy is released during the reaction.

Why does needing less activation energy help organisms? Without enzymes, many reactions would need lots of heat to happen quickly, and that much heat would damage cells. Enzymes let reactions like digesting food and making ATP happen fast enough at normal body temperature to keep you alive.

Catalysts that cause chemical change

A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up or changed by it. The substrate changes into something new (a chemical change). The enzyme comes out exactly the same, so it can grab the next substrate and do it again, thousands of times.

A chemical change means new substances are made. Slide 6's examples: fireworks, frying eggs, photosynthesis, burning wood, burning propane and digestion. Digestion is the one enzymes do inside you.

Active site and substrate

Enzyme examples and the -ase ending

Your slide highlights the end of every name: amylase, lipase, protease, lactase. Enzyme names usually end in -ase, and the first part often tells you the substrate the enzyme works on.

EnzymeWorks on (substrate)What it does
LactaseLactose (milk sugar)Breaks lactose into glucose + galactose so you can digest milk products
AmylaseStarchBreaks starch into sugars; it's in your saliva Extra
LipaseLipids (fats)Breaks fats into fatty acids and glycerol Extra
ProteaseProteinsBreaks proteins into amino acids Extra
ATP synthaseBuilds ATPMakes ATP, the chemical your cells use for energy

Notice the pattern: digestive enzymes break big molecules back down into their building blocks, the reverse of Lesson 1's monomers → polymers. Starch becomes sugars, fats become fatty acids and glycerol, and proteins become amino acids. Extra

-ose or -ase? Names ending in -ose are sugars (lactose, glucose, maltose). Names ending in -ase are enzymes (lactase, amylase). Often the enzyme is just the sugar's name with -ase: lactose → lactase, maltose → maltase, sucrose → sucrase. Practice it in the Lab.

Temperature and pH

Activity vs. temperature
Activity vs. pH

Each enzyme has a temperature and a pH where it works fastest, called its optimum, which is the peak of each graph. Move away from the peak in either direction and the enzyme slows down. Human enzymes peak near body temperature, 37 °C. Extra

Too cold · slide 9

Enzymes become inactive. Molecules move slowly, so the enzyme barely works, but its shape is fine. Warm it back up and it works again.

Too hot · slides 9 and 11

Enzymes get denatured. Your slides show this happening with heat above 40 °C. The shape is ruined, and cooling it down doesn't fix it.

The pH graph on slide 10 peaks at the enzyme's optimum pH (about pH 8 for the one pictured). Different enzymes have different optimum pH values, depending on where in the body they work.

What these graphs show (packet p. 17): enzyme activity rises to a peak at the best temperature or pH, then drops on the other side. With temperature, it drops very fast once the enzyme gets denatured.

Denatured enzymes

Denatured means the enzyme has lost its shape: the protein chain unfolds. The active site gets bent out of shape, so the substrate can no longer bind, and the enzyme can't speed up the reaction anymore. It's like trying to use a key in a lock that has melted.

Cooking an egg is denaturing: the clear egg white turns solid and white, and it never goes back. Too much heat usually denatures an enzyme for good. Extra

Try it yourself with the temperature and pH sliders in the Lab.

Common mix-ups

  • Enzymes don't add energy. They lower the energy needed to start the reaction.
  • Enzymes aren't used up. The substrate changes; the enzyme is reused.
  • Denatured doesn't mean broken into pieces. It means the shape changed, so the active site no longer fits.
  • Substrate vs. product. The substrate goes in; the products come out.
  • Inactive vs. denatured. Cold makes enzymes inactive (slow, but undamaged). Heat above 40 °C denatures them.
  • -ose vs. -ase. Lactose is the sugar (substrate); lactase is the enzyme.
